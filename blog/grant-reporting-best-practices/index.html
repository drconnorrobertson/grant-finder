Grant Reporting: Reconcile Activity Counts and Evidence

Original publication July 27, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · Website editorial

A grant report should make it possible to trace a statement back to the records and definitions that support it. Begin with the actual funder's reporting instructions, the reporting period and the approved project context. Then separate activity delivered, people counted, feedback received and any outcome evidence. A strong-looking number is not useful if the reviewer cannot tell what it counts or which period it describes.

This guide focuses on preparing a clear evidence packet and narrative for the appropriate reviewer. It uses an invented workshop example to show how attendance events, unique participants and survey responses produce different totals. It does not determine an award's compliance requirements, interpret a grant agreement or promise renewal funding. The actual award terms, current reporting instructions and responsible professional review govern a real report.

Start with the report that is actually due

Identify the award or funding reference, report type, covered period and source of the instructions. An annual narrative, an interim progress update and a financial report may ask different questions. Do not copy a previous application's attachments into a report folder and assume the same requirements apply. Keep the current request and any relevant clarification available to the people preparing the report.

Record which dates describe program activity and which describe the submission process. The end of the reporting period is not automatically the submission deadline, and an internal review date is not a funder-approved extension. Use the actual instructions to establish those dates. If a time zone or submission method is unclear, route a precise question through the appropriate channel rather than inventing a universal deadline convention.

List the requested sections in a simple preparation sheet. For each section, name the person who can supply the source records, the person who can review the interpretation and the current draft location. The sheet should organize work without reproducing private participant records. A report coordinator needs to know where the evidence is maintained and who can verify it, not necessarily receive every underlying personal detail in an email attachment.

Define the measure before collecting the total

Write the counting rule for each number that will appear in the narrative. Workshops delivered, attendance events, unique participants and completed surveys are different measures. A participant who attends three workshops contributes three attendance events but may represent one unique person under the chosen definition. If the report asks for unique participants, adding workshop headcounts without resolving overlap can produce the wrong answer even when each individual headcount is accurate.

Use the actual funder's definition when one is provided. If the definition is not clear, preserve the question and seek clarification rather than silently selecting a favorable interpretation. An internal working definition can help identify the issue, but it should remain labeled as provisional until the appropriate reviewer resolves it. Keep the definition beside the result so it does not disappear when a number is copied into a slide or summary.

Describe the boundary of the count. A workshop may have staff, volunteers and participants present, but the requested participant measure may not include all of them. A registration count may include people who did not attend. An attendance record may include someone whose eligibility status has not been verified. Do not substitute one of these source totals for another simply because the available number is larger or easier to obtain.

Reconcile the fictional attendance example

Imagine a fictional project delivers three workshops. Workshop A records twelve participant attendances, Workshop B records fifteen and Workshop C records thirteen. Adding those counts produces forty attendance events. The underlying invented roster contains twenty-five distinct practice identifiers across the three workshops. The project can therefore describe forty attendance events and twenty-five unique participants under the example's stated identifier rule, while keeping the measures separate.

Suppose the project also has a goal stated as thirty unique participants for the period. Twenty-five divided by thirty is approximately eighty-three point three percent. Forty divided by thirty would be approximately one hundred thirty-three point three percent, but that calculation mixes attendance events with a unique-person goal. The arithmetic would be possible and the conclusion would be wrong. Match the numerator and denominator definitions before presenting a progress percentage.

The example's participant identifiers are fictional labels such as P01, not real names or personal records. In a real project, use the organization's appropriate method for matching records and protecting participant information. This article does not prescribe an identity system or resolve privacy obligations. If overlap cannot be reliably resolved, state the limitation and obtain the relevant review rather than claiming a unique-person count from incomplete evidence.

Keep an explanation of corrections. If a duplicate row is identified after the initial total, preserve the original calculation and the reason for the revised number according to the organization's process. Do not delete records merely to make the data fit a target. A reviewer should be able to follow the change from the source record to the final total and understand whether it affects other sections of the report.

Keep feedback response counts attached to their denominators

In a second fictional part of the example, ten of the twenty-five unique participants return an optional feedback form, and eight of those ten select a favorable response to a particular question. Eight out of ten is eighty percent of respondents. It is not evidence that eighty percent of all twenty-five participants selected that response, because fifteen did not return that form. The response count belongs next to the percentage.

Use the actual question wording and response options when explaining what the result means. Satisfaction with a workshop is not the same as a demonstrated change in knowledge, behavior or longer-term conditions. A favorable response can be useful feedback without establishing a broader outcome. Do not promote a convenient survey item into a claim it was not designed to support, especially when the audience did not see the original instrument.

Explain who was invited to respond and when the feedback was collected. A form sent only to one workshop group does not represent all project participants automatically. A response collected immediately after a session answers a different question from a later follow-up. The report can describe these boundaries plainly without apologizing for every limitation. The objective is an accurate account of the evidence, not the appearance of a perfect data set.

Distinguish delivered activity from demonstrated change

CDC's Program Evaluation Framework emphasizes credible evidence, supported conclusions and transparency in evaluation. It was developed in a public-health context and is not a substitute for an individual funder's reporting requirements. Its general emphasis is useful here: choose evidence that fits the question and explain the limits of the conclusion. This invented reporting workflow is not a CDC-reviewed project or a validated evaluation design.

Delivering three workshops is an activity record. Counting twenty-five unique participants describes reach under the stated rule. Eight favorable survey responses describe the responses to a particular question among the people who answered it. None of those facts alone demonstrates that the workshops caused a lasting change in participants' circumstances. If the report asks about outcomes, use the relevant evidence and evaluation approach rather than renaming activity totals as impact.

A narrative can be constructive without overstating the result. Explain what was delivered, what the available evidence suggests, what remains unknown and what the team plans to examine next. Distinguish a planned evaluation step from evidence already collected. A sentence about future follow-up should not imply that the follow-up has happened, and an intended benefit should not be written as an observed result.

Build the evidence trail for each narrative statement

For a material statement, keep a reference to the source, the calculation or review performed and the person responsible for checking it. A paragraph about workshop reach might refer to the attendance reconciliation and the definition note. A paragraph about participant feedback might refer to the question wording, response counts and collection period. The narrative need not expose private underlying records to make its evidence traceable to an authorized reviewer.

Use short evidence notes instead of copying entire documents into every draft. Identify the exact table, tab, date or section where the supporting information can be found through the approved process. A link to a large folder called reports is less helpful than a reference to a particular reviewed summary. Check access for the intended reviewer without making private source material publicly accessible merely to simplify the handoff.

Mark unsupported draft statements before editing for style. If the source shows three sessions delivered, a draft claim that the project transformed the community needs evidence beyond a more polished sentence. The correct editing task may be to narrow the claim or obtain appropriate supporting information. Language quality cannot repair an evidence gap, and an AI-generated summary should not be treated as another independent source for the same unsupported assertion.

Reconcile the narrative and financial review without merging roles

Program delivery records and financial records can describe related events without using identical categories. The report coordinator should identify where the narrative refers to spending or resources and route those statements to the responsible financial reviewer. This guide does not decide allowable costs, expense recognition, cost allocation or the treatment of a particular transaction. Those questions require the actual award context and appropriate review.

Avoid explaining a financial difference solely from a program headcount. A lower participant count does not prove that a project underspent by the same percentage, and a full budget spent does not prove every activity target was met. Keep the actual records and explanations separate, then connect them where the evidence supports a relationship. Do not invent a causal explanation because two numbers moved in the same direction.

If the funder requests both a narrative and a financial document, check the references between them. A narrative that says a planned activity was deferred should not simultaneously describe it elsewhere as completed. The financial reviewer may need to examine the related entries under the actual reporting requirements. The coordinator's role is to surface the inconsistency and obtain the appropriate answer, not to create an accounting adjustment to make the story simpler.

Describe variance from the plan with useful detail

When a result differs from a stated target, identify the measure, period and size of the difference before explaining it. In the fictional example, twenty-five unique participants against a goal of thirty leaves a difference of five people on that measure. That is distinct from the forty attendance events across three sessions. Keeping the measures separate prevents a strong activity total from obscuring the specific reach target that was not met in the example.

Use documented explanations rather than convenient speculation. If the team has records showing that a session date changed, report that fact and its source. Do not claim the change caused the entire participant difference unless the evidence supports that conclusion. It is acceptable to say that the reason remains under review. The next action might be to examine registration and attendance patterns, not to assert an unmeasured explanation as settled fact.

Separate corrective plans from promises. The team may propose changing outreach or scheduling for the next period, but a proposed action is not evidence of future success. State what the organization intends to review or implement through the appropriate process. Any change requiring funder approval should be handled under the actual award instructions; the report narrative itself does not authorize a scope or budget amendment.

Review the report as a connected document

Read the executive summary after the detailed sections are reconciled. Summaries often retain an earlier number after a table has changed. Search for the key measures and check every occurrence against the reviewed result. A corrected participant total in the body is not enough if the opening paragraph still uses the old count or a chart label still calls attendance events unique people.

Check dates, units and rounding across the document. If the narrative uses whole people and the percentage uses one decimal place, state the basis consistently. Do not round a count of people into a different observed count. A percentage may be rounded for presentation while the underlying numerator and denominator remain explicit. The report should allow a reviewer to reproduce the calculation without guessing which version of a number was used.

Review claims in captions and headings as well as body text. A chart titled proven impact can overstate evidence even when its footnote correctly describes participant feedback. A photograph caption can imply an activity occurred during the reporting period when the image comes from an earlier event. Keep the presentation aligned with the actual evidence and the organization's permissions for using images or participant statements.

Preserve the reviewed version and the actual submission evidence

Before submission, identify the version the authorized reviewer approved and the files that belong to it. Keep that version separate from later working edits. This guide does not prescribe a universal sign-off chain; use the organization and funder's actual process. A coordinator should be able to tell which narrative, tables and attachments were included without reconstructing the packet from several private inboxes.

After the actual submission step, preserve the relevant receipt or confirmation according to the process in use. A file saved locally, an upload attempted and a report accepted are different states. Do not describe a report as accepted merely because someone clicked a button or sent an internal email. If the system or funder requires another step, keep it visible and assign the next action to the appropriate person.

The fictional CSV supports the attendance and feedback examples in this article. It contains workshop memberships for twenty-five invented identifiers, forty attendance events and the ten invented feedback responses. It is a teaching file, not client data or evidence of a funded project's performance. Use it to practice reconciling measures, then apply the relevant definitions, instructions and privacy process to any real reporting work.

Keep a small correction log through final review

When several people review a report, distinguish a wording suggestion from a change to the underlying evidence. A reviewer may improve a sentence without changing the count it describes. Another reviewer may discover that an event was outside the reporting period. Record a material evidence correction with its source and identify the affected narrative, table and chart references. This prevents a corrected table from coexisting with an older summary that still repeats the original claim.

Do not treat every reviewer comment as authorization to change the project record. If a suggested number conflicts with the source, route the discrepancy to the person responsible for that evidence. A comment such as use the larger count needs an explanation of the measure and supporting records before it can be accepted. The report should reflect the reviewed evidence, not whichever suggestion appears latest in the document's comment thread.

After resolving a correction, check whether it changes a denominator or interpretation elsewhere. Removing an out-of-period participant may affect a unique-person count, a progress percentage and the stated population eligible for a feedback form. The proper correction is not merely replacing one numeral. Trace the consequences through the report and preserve the reason for the revision so the final reviewer understands what changed.

Use the correction log as an internal preparation aid, with access appropriate to the records it references. It is not an additional funder-required form unless the actual instructions say so. The purpose is to help the organization produce one coherent reviewed account, including a clear distinction between a proposed edit, an accepted evidence correction and the final submitted version.

Frequently asked questions

Can I add workshop headcounts to report unique participants?

Only if the resulting method matches the requested definition and correctly handles overlap. In the fictional example, twelve, fifteen and thirteen attendances sum to forty events, while the underlying identifiers represent twenty-five distinct people. Those are different measures. Use the actual reporting definition and appropriate record-matching process rather than presenting the sum of event headcounts as a unique-person count by default.

Does a favorable survey percentage describe everyone served?

Not automatically. Eight favorable responses out of ten returned forms describes those ten respondents to that question. It does not establish the same result among all twenty-five fictional participants. Keep the response count, question wording, collection timing and relevant limitations beside the percentage. A response measure should not be expanded into a broader outcome or causal claim without the appropriate evidence.

Does this guide replace the funder's reporting instructions?

No. The actual award terms, report request, current instructions and appropriate review govern a real report. This article organizes evidence and illustrates counting differences. It does not determine compliance, allowable costs, submission deadlines or whether a particular change requires approval. Confirm those questions through the relevant official process rather than treating a general worksheet as authority.

What should I do when a target was not met?

State the actual measure, period and difference, then provide the documented explanation and remaining questions. Keep related activity totals separate so they do not conceal the target variance. Proposed next actions can be described as plans, without promising results or implying that the report itself authorizes changes to the award. Use the actual instructions for any required approval or additional explanation.

Can AI write the report from the source packet?

AI may assist an authorized drafting process, but its output still needs checking against the actual records and definitions. Do not upload private information to an unapproved tool or treat fluent text as supporting evidence. Verify totals, qualifiers, dates and claims with the responsible people. The organization retains responsibility for its actual review and submission process, including identifying unsupported statements before they enter the final report.

Does a good report guarantee renewal funding?

No. A clear report can explain the work and evidence accurately, but this guide provides no basis for promising a renewal or predicting a funder's decision. Avoid turning reporting advice into claims about funding outcomes. The practical objective is a traceable, appropriately reviewed account of the relevant period that distinguishes observations, limitations and proposed next steps.
