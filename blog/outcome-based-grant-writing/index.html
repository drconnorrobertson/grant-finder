Outcome-Based Grant Writing: Connect Activities, Changes and Evidence

Outcome-based grant writing explains the change a program intends to support and how the organization would learn whether that change occurred. It also explains what the organization will actually deliver. A proposal needs both parts. Counting workshops does not establish that participants learned something, while promising learning without describing the teaching activity leaves the reader unable to follow the proposal. The practical task is to connect delivery, intended change and evidence without turning a reasonable expectation into a guaranteed result.

This guide works through an entirely fictional adult document-reading workshop. Every participant count, target, observation and resource amount below is invented for teaching. There is no underlying client program, award, participant dataset or evaluation. The example shows how to write definitions and identify missing evidence; it does not establish that this workshop would work. Use the actual opportunity instructions to decide which sections and measures belong in a real application. A useful planning model does not override a funder's required format.

Start with the decision the description must support

Before drawing boxes, write the question your description should help someone answer. In the fictional example, the planning question is whether a small workshop can be delivered as designed and whether a simple assessment can describe participants' ability to find information in a sample document. Those are two related questions. Neither asks whether a workshop causes long-term financial security, and neither can answer that much larger question merely because financial security appears in the organization's mission.

The program team would need to agree what information-reading task matters, whether the task is appropriate for participants and whether the proposed assessment is understandable. A grant writer cannot settle those questions by choosing a persuasive adjective. Talk through the intended use with the people who would deliver the program and, where appropriate, those expected to participate. Record disagreements that affect the design. If one person imagines a legal document and another imagines a library notice, the apparent agreement on reading skills conceals different programs.

CDC's program-description guidance presents logic models as ways to communicate relationships between activities and intended changes. Its evaluation-design guidance separately asks what questions the evaluation should answer and how findings would be used. Those general ideas help organize this example. CDC's public-health guidance is not an endorsement of this fictional workshop, proof of grant eligibility or evidence that a particular funder will reward this structure. The instructions and evidence for a real program still need their own review.

Separate the need from the proposed solution

A need statement describes the issue the program is addressing. An activity statement describes what the organization proposes to do about it. Mixing the two can make the proposed activity appear necessary simply because it has been named. For example, saying that a community needs six workshops does not explain the underlying difficulty. It may only restate the delivery plan. A more useful planning question is what people find difficult about locating and interpreting information, and what evidence supports that description.

In this teaching case, the need remains a hypothesis: some intended participants may want practice identifying dates, conditions and contact details in everyday notices. No local prevalence figure is asserted. A real application might have relevant participant feedback, an appropriately scoped needs assessment or other evidence. Describe the source, population and date rather than substituting a national figure for local evidence without explanation. If the evidence is incomplete, say what is unknown and how that uncertainty affects the proposed scope.

Avoid converting an understandable problem into a claim about every member of a group. People may have different language preferences, reading experiences, access needs or reasons for participating. Those differences can affect the activity and the interpretation of results. A planning narrative can acknowledge them without collecting intrusive personal information. Specify which differences matter to the question and what adaptation would be considered. Do not promise that one workshop format meets every need or that a completed assessment captures every useful form of learning.

Name the resources before promising the activity

Inputs are the resources available or required to deliver an activity. For the fictional workshop, imagine one facilitator, a room, accessible teaching materials and time to prepare and review the assessment. These are resources, not achievements. A room booking does not demonstrate learning. Staff time can support delivery, but hours allocated on a spreadsheet do not establish that qualified staff are available. In a real proposal, distinguish resources already available from those contingent on an award or another agreement.

The invented plan provides six sessions, each lasting ninety minutes. That is nine session hours. Suppose the facilitator also needs twelve preparation hours and six review hours. The total illustrated facilitator allocation is twenty-seven hours. These figures describe the teaching example only; they are not recommended staffing levels. The important writing move is making the components visible. If a budget includes only nine hours, the narrative and budget are describing different amounts of work unless preparation and review are supported elsewhere.

Link the input description to the separate budget rather than repeating a long cost table in every section. Identify the assumption that drives each quantity: sessions, preparation time, materials or participant support. If the opportunity has specific rules about allowable costs, consult those rules independently. A coherent logic model does not make an expense allowable. Similarly, describing a partner as an input does not establish that the partner has committed resources. Obtain the actual agreement before presenting a contribution as confirmed.

Describe activities so another person can picture delivery

An activity is something the program does. In the example, the facilitator introduces a sample notice, demonstrates how to locate its date and conditions, guides practice with a second notice and invites participants to try another task independently. That description gives more information than simply saying participants will be empowered. It also helps identify what would need preparation and what evidence might show whether the session was delivered as planned. Keep operating detail proportionate to the application question.

A delivery description should identify the intended participants, setting, timing and scope without inventing operational readiness. If registration procedures or accessible formats are not yet settled, make that a planning dependency. Do not quietly turn a proposed arrangement into an established service. In the fictional case, six sessions are planned and twenty-four people are invited to one cohort. The plan has no real invitation list, venue or delivery date. Those invented quantities are used only to explain the arithmetic and terminology.

Explain meaningful variations before they complicate interpretation. If some sessions use a different document type, the resulting assessment may not be directly comparable. If a participant attends only the final session, their exposure differs from someone who attends all six. That does not automatically make one person's experience invalid. It means the description should preserve the difference. Decide what question each record can answer instead of treating every person who touched the program as having received an identical service.

Count outputs without calling them outcomes

Outputs describe what an activity produces or delivers. In the fictional plan, six delivered sessions would be an output. Eighteen people attending at least one session would describe reach. Seventy-two attendance records would count participant visits, not seventy-two different people. All three numbers could be useful, but they answer different questions. Write the unit beside the number every time it matters. A spreadsheet column headed participants should not contain attendance records unless the definition explicitly means visits rather than unique people.

The fictional worksheet uses twenty-four invited people, eighteen unique attendees, seventy-two attendance records and twelve people attending at least four sessions. Eighteen divided by twenty-four is seventy-five percent of invited people attending at least once. Seventy-two divided by eighteen is four recorded visits per attendee on average. That average does not mean every attendee came four times. Twelve divided by eighteen is about sixty-six point seven percent of attendees meeting the invented four-session threshold. The threshold is a teaching definition, not an established standard.

Define completion in advance if the term will be used. Attendance at four sessions might be one administrative definition, while finishing an assessment is another. Neither automatically proves mastery. If the narrative calls both groups completers, a reviewer cannot tell which denominator supports the outcome percentage. Use descriptive labels such as attended at least four sessions and completed both assessments. The longer label is often more informative than a short category whose meaning changes between the application, database and final report.

Write outcomes as intended changes with a defined scope

An outcome concerns a change in a person, organization or condition that the program intends to support. In this example, the proposed short-term outcome is improved performance on a particular document-reading task. That is narrower than becoming financially secure or making better decisions in every setting. A bounded outcome can still be valuable. Describe the task and timing clearly enough that someone can see what would count as evidence, while avoiding claims beyond what the assessment actually measures.

Imagine a four-item teaching assessment scored from zero to four. The fictional planning target is that ten of fourteen people completing both the initial and final assessment increase their score by at least one point. Ten divided by fourteen is about seventy-one point four percent. This is an invented target, not an observed improvement rate or a validated prediction. In a real proposal, explain the basis for the target. It might be a preliminary planning assumption that requires revision rather than a number supported by prior results.

Do not manufacture a track record to make a target sound more credible. A statement that prior cohorts achieved a particular result requires actual records and an appropriate comparison of definitions. If no prior program exists, describe the program as new. If a different program provides relevant research, explain the differences in participants, setting and delivery. Evidence from another context can inform a hypothesis without becoming your organization's historical performance. Keep borrowed research, internal observations and proposed targets visibly separate.

Show the proposed pathway and its assumptions

A simple pathway for the fictional example is resources supporting six teaching sessions, sessions providing practice, and practice potentially supporting better performance on the defined task. The arrows represent expectations to examine. They are not a causal finding. Each connection rests on assumptions: suitable materials, understandable instructions, meaningful participation and an assessment sensitive to the skill being practiced. Naming these assumptions helps reveal where the plan needs further development before the narrative promises a result.

Consider what might interrupt the pathway. A change of venue may affect attendance. Materials may be difficult to use in the intended format. Participants may already know the task, leaving little room for a higher score. A different notice at the final assessment may be harder for reasons unrelated to learning. These are not excuses to add after results are known. They are design questions to consider in advance and context to preserve when interpreting what happened.

Longer-term goals can appear in a model if their relationship to the activity is explained honestly. The organization might hope that stronger document-reading skills support confidence in everyday information tasks. It should not claim that the workshop establishes employment retention, financial stability or community-wide change without evidence suited to those questions. Distinguish the goal the organization contributes toward from the change this particular evaluation can describe. Several other influences may matter more over a longer period than a short teaching activity.

Match each question to an evidence plan

A process question might ask whether the six sessions occurred and whether the planned practice activity was included. A delivery log and appropriately defined attendance records could help answer that question. An outcome question might ask how paired assessment scores differ from the beginning to the end. That requires a way to connect the two assessments for the same person, a scoring definition and a plan for missing records. A list of completed sessions cannot substitute for the paired score evidence.

For each proposed measure, write down the unit, population, collection timing, source and limitation. In the fictional case, an attendance unit is one recorded person-session, while an assessment unit is one person's score at a defined point. The paired analysis includes only people with both scores. The invited population is larger. These differences should be visible before a percentage is written. If collection procedures cannot support the intended comparison, revise the question or the evidence plan rather than promising an unavailable result.

Ask how the measure will be interpreted, not only how it will be collected. A one-point increase on an invented four-item exercise is a change on that exercise. It does not establish a validated level of literacy or explain why the change occurred. Repeated exposure to the task, different scoring practices and unrelated experiences could influence the result. Decide whether the assessment needs further development and whether an evaluation specialist should help with the actual design. This guide supplies no validated assessment instrument.

Keep missing data and denominators visible

Suppose the invented post-delivery records contain eighteen unique attendees, fourteen people with both assessments, ten with a higher score, three with the same score and one with a lower score. Ten plus three plus one equals fourteen paired records. Four attendees are outside that paired comparison. Saying that seventy-one point four percent of all participants improved would hide the difference between paired respondents and attendees. The accurate teaching statement identifies the fourteen people with both scores and separately reports the four missing pairs.

Ten divided by eighteen is about fifty-five point six percent of attendees with a documented increase in the paired records. That is a different statement from claiming the other eight did not improve. Four had no paired result, and four had an unchanged or lower score in the invented records. Do not silently code missing observations as zero improvement unless a justified, explicitly described method calls for it. A missing response describes what is unknown, not necessarily what occurred.

When comparing periods, preserve the definitions as well as the totals. If an earlier cohort used a two-point improvement threshold and a later cohort used one point, the percentages do not describe the same outcome. If one period includes only attendees with paired scores and another includes everyone invited, the denominator also changed. Explain those differences before placing the figures side by side. A visually tidy chart can conceal an incompatible comparison just as easily as an ambiguous sentence can.

Distinguish outcome description from causal impact

CDC's evaluation guidance distinguishes observing intended outcomes from evaluating whether activities caused them. That distinction matters in proposal language. A before-and-after score comparison can describe a change among the people measured. On its own, it does not show what would have happened without the workshop. Calling every observed change impact can imply a stronger conclusion than the design supports. Explain the actual question and design instead of relying on a label whose meaning readers may interpret differently.

The fictional example therefore uses wording such as a documented score increase among paired assessment participants. It does not say the workshop caused ten people to improve. A causal question would require an appropriate design and careful consideration of feasibility, ethics and assumptions. Adding a comparison group label to a spreadsheet would not by itself settle those issues. A real organization should develop that design with suitable expertise and the people affected, rather than borrowing a technical term to strengthen a narrative.

Write limitations where readers encounter the result. A distant disclaimer does not repair a headline claiming proven effectiveness. If evidence is preliminary, the main sentence should say so. If a measurement captures only a narrow task, name that task. This approach also helps the organization use findings internally. A result can inform revisions to materials or collection procedures without establishing the program's overall effectiveness. Useful learning does not require every observation to become a public claim of success.

Turn the model into a concise proposal paragraph

An initial draft might say the program will transform community outcomes by training twenty-four people. That sentence blends invitation capacity, delivery and an unsupported promise. A more useful fictional planning paragraph would explain that the proposed six-session workshop offers practice locating dates and conditions in sample notices, describes how attendance would be recorded, and identifies a paired four-item assessment as a proposed learning measure. It would state that the target is provisional and that the assessment does not establish causation or long-term community change.

After writing the paragraph, compare it with the budget and timeline. Six sessions in the narrative should not become eight room bookings without an explanation. A final assessment scheduled before the last practice session may answer a different question from the one intended. Follow-up collection requires time and responsibility even if the application does not provide a separate box for it. Fix contradictions in the underlying plan before polishing the wording. Clear prose cannot reconcile two incompatible delivery assumptions.

Keep the final application readable by moving detailed definitions to the appropriate attachment when instructions permit. A short model, an evidence table and a narrative serve different purposes. They should agree on population, timing and measures, but they do not need identical sentences repeated in every location. Before submission, check the actual opportunity's instructions, word limits and required attachments. No model in this guide guarantees eligibility, a favorable review, funding or permission to collect particular participant information.

Frequently asked questions

Can outputs and outcomes both belong in a proposal?

Yes, when relevant to the instructions and program. Outputs describe delivery or reach, while outcomes concern intended changes. Six sessions and an assessment score change answer different questions. Preserve both definitions rather than treating an activity count as proof of learning or deleting delivery detail because the proposal also discusses change.

What if the program has no prior results?

Describe it as proposed or new and identify the basis and uncertainty of any target. Do not invent a historical cohort. Relevant research may inform planning, but distinguish that research from your own records and explain differences in setting or participants. A target is not an observation merely because it contains a precise percentage.

Does a logic-model arrow prove causation?

No. In a planning model, the arrow expresses an expected relationship to examine. Whether an activity caused a result requires evidence and a design appropriate to that question. The fictional before-and-after assessment here illustrates descriptive measurement and limitations; it is not a causal evaluation or validated teaching intervention.

Which denominator should an improvement percentage use?

Use the population defined by the question and available evidence, and state it explicitly. Ten increases among fourteen paired assessments is different from ten documented increases among eighteen attendees. Report missing pairs separately. Neither wording permits an assumption about unobserved changes among people without both assessment records.

Must every proposal use the example's four-session completion rule?

No. That rule is invented solely to demonstrate a definition. The real program and opportunity may use different participation requirements or none. Define any threshold deliberately, explain its purpose and preserve it consistently across planning and reporting. Meeting an attendance threshold does not automatically demonstrate competence or an outcome.

How should a team use the downloadable worksheet?

Use it to practice recognizing units and denominators before preparing a real program description. Its rows are authored teaching figures, not participant records, evidence or a recommended target. Replace assumptions only in an appropriate separate planning process with verified information, suitable review and the actual application requirements. Do not present the fictional numbers as your organization's results.
